- **Core idea:** The j-th unit of product i has marginal cost `(2j - 1) * P_i`, and buying k units costs `P_i * k^2`. The optimal purchase is the cheapest marginal units globally.
- **Threshold predicate:** For a cutoff X, product i contributes `t = (X // P_i + 1) // 2` units (odd multiples up to X) and cost `P_i * t^2`. This predicate is monotone in X.
- **Final remainder:** Binary search the largest feasible integer X. Because the cost function is a step function, X+1 must be the next marginal cost. Spend the remaining budget on `remaining // (X+1)` tied units. This is safe because `C(X+1) > M`, so the remainder is strictly less than the total cost of all units at X+1.
- **Upper bounds:** Use two safe infeasible highs and take the minimum. From the cheapest product: `k = isqrt(M // minP) + 1`, `high = (2k - 1) * minP`. From all products: `S = sum(P)`, `k = isqrt(M // S) + 1`, `high = (2k - 1) * maxP`; at that X every product contributes at least k units, so cost is at least `k^2 * S > M`.
- **Implementation details:** Sort P ascending. In the predicate, break when `P_i > mid` because later products contribute zero, and break early when cost exceeds M. Use bit shift for division by 2. Keep all products (including P_i > M); they naturally contribute zero until their first marginal cost and then make the predicate infeasible.
- **Complexity:** Sorting is O(N log N). Binary search is O(N log H), where H is at most about 9e13 and often much smaller due to the all-products bound; worst-case operations are around 8e6, acceptable in PyPy. Memory is O(N).
- **Edge cases:** Answer can be 0. X can be 0, especially when many products have first marginal cost 1 and M is smaller than their count. Flat feasible regions are handled by searching over integer X, so X+1 is still the next marginal cost. Sample 1 gives X=4, count=3, remainder=1, answer=3. Sample 2 gives X=37, count=53, answer=53.
