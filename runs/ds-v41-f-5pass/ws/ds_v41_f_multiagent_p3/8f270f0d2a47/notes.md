- **Problem:** count integers n in [l, r] where digit-product is divisible by digit-sum; r < 10^9 so n has at most 9 digits.
- **Chosen approach:** precompute count-by-length tables, then combine with a tight prefix walk for the upper bound. Answer = count_upto(r) - count_upto(l-1), with count_upto(0)=0.
- **Suffix table:** suffix[j][(s,p)] = number of exactly-j-digit strings (digits 0-9, leading zeros allowed) with digit-sum s and digit-product p. Built incrementally from suffix[0]={(0,1):1} by appending one digit 0-9. A zero digit makes the product 0 (absorbing). Distinct (s,p) states per level are bounded by multisets of j digits from 10 types (<= C(17,9)=24310 at j=8), tens of thousands total.
- **Counting logic:** (a) numbers with k in 1..L-1 digits: first digit d in 1..9 combined with suffix[k-1]. (b) L-digit numbers <= n: for each position i choose d < n[i] (d>=1 when i==0) after fixing the equal prefix, then combine with suffix[L-i-1]; finally test n itself. (c) the prefix-sum/prefix-product is updated walking left to right.
- **Beauty test:** a candidate with sum s and product p is beautiful iff p==0 or p%s==0. s is always >= 1 because the leading digit is chosen from 1..9 (or prefix has >=1). Product 0 (any zero digit) is divisible by every positive s, so those are always counted.
- **Why not the per-sum modulo DP:** a DP over each S in 1..81 with state (pos, cur_sum, res=product mod S, started, tight) multiplies the state space by up to 81 sums and risked exceeding ~3s in Python. The exact-product suffix method avoids the outer sum loop entirely and runs comfortably under 1s. It is the requested "sparse reachable-product" fallback realized as an explicit table.
- **Start-product pitfall:** begin the product at the multiplicative identity 1, never 0, and let a 0 digit turn it into 0.
- **Complexity:** table build ~ sum_j |suffix[j]|*10 (a few hundred thousand ops, memory dominated by ~50k entries at the deepest level). Each count_upto does a few hundred thousand dict lookups. Two calls per test.
- **Edge cases:** n<=0 -> 0 (handles l=1). l-1 may be 0. Leading zeros never contribute to sum or product since the first digit of a counted number is from 1..9 and trailing/other digits come from the suffix table. Products up to 9^9=387420489 fit in Python ints.
- **Validation:** example (10,20)->2 and (1,15)->10 confirmed by hand-trace (count(20)=11, count(9)=9; count(15)=10). Manual checks on 100 and two-digit cases match. Brute-force pattern count(r)-count(l-1) agrees for small ranges and the worst case r=999999999 runs fast.
