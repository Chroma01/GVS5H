- **Core idea:** The j-th unit of product i has marginal cost `(2j-1)P_i`, and buying k units costs `P_i*k^2`. The optimal purchase is the prefix of the multiset of all marginal costs. Binary search a marginal-cost threshold C, take all units with marginal cost at most C, then spend the remainder on units with marginal cost C+1.
- **Threshold computation:** For threshold C, product i contributes `k_i = (C // P_i + 1) // 2` units and cost `P_i*k_i^2`. During feasibility checks, accumulate cost and stop as soon as it exceeds M.
- **Upper bound:** After filtering `P_i <= M`, let `pmin`, `last`, `total_sum`, and `cnt_min` be the minimum P, maximum P, sum of P, and count of minimum P. Two guaranteed unaffordable thresholds are:
  - `pmin * (2k-1)` where `k = isqrt(M // (pmin*cnt_min)) + 1`; the cheapest products alone already exceed M.
  - `last * (2k-1)` where `k = isqrt(M // total_sum) + 1`; every product has at least k units.
  Use the minimum as `high`; `low=0` is affordable.
- **Final remainder:** Let C be the largest affordable threshold, base count B, and base cost S. Since `cost(C+1) > M`, the remaining budget `R = M-S` is less than `A*(C+1)`, where A is the number of next units with marginal cost C+1. Therefore `floor(R/(C+1))` such units can be bought, and no more units are possible because every remaining unit costs at least C+1.
- **Filtering:** Products with `P_i > M` can never be bought because even one unit exceeds the whole budget. Removing them preserves the answer and improves bounds and loop sizes.
- **Implementation/performance:** Sort P. Feasibility is O(N) with early breaks when `p > C` or cost exceeds M. Two loop variants avoid the `p > C` branch when all products are active. The prefix-based high bounds keep binary-search iterations small; worst-case remains O(N log U), but U is often much smaller than a generic 1e18 bound. Python integer arithmetic is safe; affordable checks stop near M.
- **Edge cases:** If no product remains after filtering, answer is 0. C can be 0, for example several `P_i=1` and M smaller than their count; then `C+1=1` and the remainder formula still works. If C lies in a gap between marginal costs, `C+1` is exactly the next marginal cost because otherwise `cost(C+1)=cost(C)`.
